# Auditing a genealogy research effort

Stalled research usually is not short of effort. It is short of **one of the checks below**. Run them in order.

## 1. The corpus-monoculture check — do this first

**Ask: how many distinct archives has this research actually searched?**

Long efforts routinely run hundreds of searches against a single platform and then conclude "this is the edge of the archive." It is usually the edge of *that platform*. Before accepting any wall as structural, list the corpora tried and the corpora that exist. The first step outside a monoculture very often produces an immediate hit — or exposes a bug that was silently voiding searches.

## 2. Which negatives are void?

A negative is only as good as the query that produced it. Mark a prior negative **void** — not "weak", void — if it was run:

- on **one spelling** only (z/s, y/i, ph/f, accents, hyphenated place names)
- on **one name form** for a person the records garble
- **without an OCR/index diagnostic** proving the corpus was searchable
- inside a **date window later proved wrong**
- with a filter that **silently does not apply** to that search mode

⚠ This is usually the largest single category of recoverable work. Re-running void negatives is cheap and has a real hit rate.

## 3. Source counts are a diagnostic

Pull every dead end — a person with neither parent attached — and **sort by attached source count**:

| Sources | Reading |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | A genuinely thin person. Probably stays walled |
| **20+, no parents** | ★ **A documentation failure, not a records failure.** Somebody's parents were never linked. This is your best target |

A heavily-sourced person with no parents is the highest-yield item on almost any tree. Re-run the scan after any session that adds parents and the queue re-sorts itself.

## 4. Placeholder dates presented as facts

Look hard at any date that is suspiciously round, sits oddly against siblings, or **matches a different event**. Recurring pattern: a child's "birth year" that is actually **the year the parents married**, entered by a contributor from a marriage record.

Test: does the arithmetic work against every other date on the profile? A father born the year his parents wed cannot have a child sixteen years later. **Check the arithmetic before building anything on a date.**

## 5. Misattached sources

On any profile with many sources, verify a sample actually belongs to that person — name, year, spouse, place. Sources migrate to same-named siblings constantly.

When you find one: **leave a note setting out the evidence rather than detaching it.** Moving another contributor's work is their call.

## 6. Duplicate people created from truncated indexes

An index that truncates a name ("Maria Soares" for "Dionizia Maria Soares") spawns stub profiles with one source and no vitals. Symptoms: a bare stub, recently created, whose single source is an index entry whose **image** carries the full name.

Same cause, different symptom: one person indexed under several garbled given names becomes several profiles. **Anchor identity on the parents' names, never the given name.**

## 7. One-document conclusions

Scan the log for claims resting on a single record — especially filiação. Reversals are normal and should be recorded as reversals, not quietly overwritten.

When a log has changed its position more than once on a question, **say so at the top of the summary** and give the current tally, so the next reader does not rebuild on a superseded conclusion.

## 8. Who has actually been searched?

List every non-family name the records attach to the family — declarants, witnesses, godparents, the person who signed for an illiterate relative, in-laws, employers, former enslavers. Then mark which have been researched.

The answer is usually *almost none*. **This is normally the biggest unexploited move in a stalled project**, because:

- the named ancestors may be people the archive was designed not to record
- the people beside them are often in the printed record
- for slavery-era research, the **enslaver's name is the index key** to the records that name the ancestor

## 9. Leads that need no archive

Before proposing more archival work, check for:

- **living relatives already named** in modern records (declarants, heirs) and never contacted
- **active online collaborators** on the same profiles, never messaged
- documents already **found but never opened**
- searches already **planned in the log** and never run

These are always the cheapest items and are routinely skipped in favour of harder ones.

## Writing the output

Rank recommendations by **cost against payoff**, not by interest. A table with the action, its cost, and what it would settle.

Be explicit about what the research **cannot** reach, and say it plainly rather than implying more work will get there. Then separate that from what it **can** now reach — a stalled project usually has more live options than its log suggests, just not the ones it has been pushing on.

Finally: **flag your own errors from earlier in the work.** A correction to a previous conclusion is worth more than a new finding, because everything built on the bad conclusion is now suspect.
